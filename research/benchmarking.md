# Benchmarking

Eight products were selected to cover the four evidence types a prospective mover can consult today — global composite rankings (QS, THE, ARWU), field-specific rankings (CSRankings), output-based indices (Nature Index, Scimago), and outcome/community evidence (LinkedIn Alumni, 1point3acres) — and each was assessed against the mobility-decision job: "where do people like me actually go, and do they stay?"

## QS World University Rankings

QS ([topuniversities.com](https://www.topuniversities.com/world-university-rankings)) is the most widely consulted annual league table, ranking 1,504 institutions across 106 systems on one composite score. Roughly 45% of that score is opinion: Academic Reputation (30%) and Employer Reputation (15%) from QS's own surveys (150k+ academic, ~99k employer responses), the rest Scopus bibliometrics and institution-submitted ratios. Interaction is a paginated sortable table with region/country/city filters, per-indicator columns, drill-down to university profiles, and a 4-way comparison tool; subject rankings live in separate products. Its strengths are legibility (one familiar number, huge brand recognition), a published methodology with exposed per-indicator scores, and strong decision scaffolding around the table. Its gaps are structural: internationalization is measured as institutional stock ratios, never trajectories; every visitor sees the same list regardless of origin; the prestige-survey core has documented volatility and conflict-of-interest criticism; and geography exists only as a filter, so country- and city-level outcomes are invisible.

## Times Higher Education WUR

THE ([timeshighereducation.com](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/world-university-rankings/latest/world-ranking)) ranks 2,191 institutions from 115 countries on 18 indicators across five pillars, built from Scopus bibliometrics (18.7M publications), a 108k-response reputation survey (33% of the score alone), and self-reported institutional data. One league table with a location filter and name search gives an answer in seconds; drill-down reaches a profile page with rank band, subject ranks, and a small stat card — no cost, admissions, or graduate-destination data (verified on the UniTrento profile). Strengths: enormous curated data foundation, breadth, near-zero interaction cost, and a rank that third parties treat as a credential. Gaps: no people-flow evidence of any kind (International Outlook, 7.5%, measures institutional shares, not movement), no origin conditioning, score differences that map to no outcome a mover experiences, and ranks that collapse into wide bands (351–400) exactly where most applicants land. No map, no city dimension, no stay/leave dynamics.

## ShanghaiRanking ARWU

ARWU ([shanghairanking.com](https://www.shanghairanking.com/rankings/arwu/2025)) is the oldest global table (2003–), ranking the top 1000 universities purely on elite research: Nobel/Fields alumni and staff (30%), Highly Cited Researchers (20%), Nature & Science papers (20%), indexed papers (20%), per-capita output (10%). No surveys, no self-reported metrics. Interaction is a flat table per year (back to 2003) with a country dropdown, institution profile pages, and Excel export; GRAS adds 57 subject tables including CS & Engineering. Strengths: 23 editions of near-unchanged methodology, fully auditable third-party data, subject granularity, zero-friction access. Gaps: no human-mobility data at all, no user conditioning (every visitor sees the identical table), decades-lagged elite prestige rather than conditions an incoming student will face, and institution-only rows with strong size bias — it answers "which large university accumulated the most elite output", never "which country or city should I move to".

## CSRankings

CSRankings ([csrankings.org](https://csrankings.org)) scores CS departments purely by faculty publication counts at selective venues: DBLP records joined to a community-curated list of PhD-advising faculty, credit split 1/N per coauthor, geometric mean across selected areas, code and data open on GitHub. The single table re-ranks live as the user toggles region, year window, and a checkbox tree of research areas; expanding an institution lists every counted faculty member with homepage/Scholar/DBLP links. Strengths: full reproducibility (the opposite of black-box surveys), fine-grained subfield and time control, and drill-down that turns the ranking into an advisor-finding tool. Gaps: it ranks paper production, not people flows — no origin country, no stay rates, no post-degree destinations; the proxy structurally underrepresents journal-oriented European and Asian systems; and geography is only a filter, never a unit of analysis. The transparent-methodology-plus-drilldown pattern is the part worth borrowing; the object of measurement is not.

## Nature Index

Nature Index ([nature.com/nature-index](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/)) counts author affiliations on articles in 178 panel-selected, survey-validated elite venues, reduced to two explainable metrics (Count, fractional Share) over a rolling 12-month window. A table generator (region, sector, subject) yields sortable position tables; institution profiles show trends, subject breakdowns, and top collaboration partners. Strengths: simple metrics with openly published methodology and explicit caveats about what the index does not measure; free and current; one-click drill-down. Gaps: it counts articles, not people — no individuals, nationality, career stage, or movement exists in the data model; nothing analogous to Arrival/Stay/No-return; the elite-journal filter underrepresents conference-dominated CS; aggregation stops at institution/country. What transfers to OSR is the packaging, not the data: a one-line explainable metric plus an honest methodology page is exactly the role of OSR's Tier and /info.

## Scimago Institutions Rankings

SIR ([scimagoir.com](https://www.scimagoir.com)) ranks 10,824 institutions across five sectors on a fixed 50/30/20 composite of Research (Scopus), Innovation (PATSTAT patents), and Societal (web visibility, media, SDG, gender, and policy-impact) indicators. One paginated table driven by dropdowns (year to 2009, sector, region/country, Scopus subject area, dimension toggle); institution profiles expose the indicator matrix. Strengths: breadth for free — it covers government labs and institutes (CNRS, Max Planck, NII) that student rankings omit; CS-within-one-country filtering; published methodology; seconds to a shortlist. Gaps: zero mobility evidence by construction; identical output for every viewer; bare ordinal positions that hide magnitude and hard-code the publisher's weighting; institution-only granularity with Scopus/English bias. SIR validates the filter-by-field-and-country table pattern while demonstrating the trap OSR must avoid: showing ranks without the magnitudes behind them.

## LinkedIn Alumni tool

The Alumni tab on every LinkedIn school page ([linkedin.com/alumni](https://www.linkedin.com/alumni)) aggregates self-reported profiles (1.1B+ members, 23,000+ institutions) into six clickable facet columns — where they live, where they work, what they do/studied/know, how you're connected — that filter conjunctively down to individual contactable profiles. Strengths: unmatched breadth and freshness, three-click answers to "which cities absorb this university's CS graduates", and the one affordance no anonymized dataset can match — drill-down ends at a real person you can message. Gaps: a snapshot, not a trajectory — only current location is shown, so stay, onward-move, and return rates cannot be derived, and the attendance-year filter has been removed; school-centric, so "where do Chinese CS students go" is unaskable and origin conditioning does not exist; raw counts conflate flows with per-country LinkedIn adoption (academia underrepresented); and, decisive for the core persona, LinkedIn exited mainland China in 2023, so the very peer cohort is both thin in the data and largely unable to reach the tool.

## 1point3acres Offer Reports + DataPoints

1point3acres ([offer.1point3acres.com](https://offer.1point3acres.com/)) is the largest overseas-Chinese applicant community's crowdsourced database: a decade of self-reported admissions (with GPA, test scores, undergrad tier), job offers with compensation, and H1B/green-card timelines, fed by a points-for-reports economy. Interaction spans forum threads and a structured browser with school/major/degree/season filters, a positioning calculator matching the user's own profile against historical admits, and side-by-side program comparison; free access is metered to 3 applicant backgrounds per 24h. Strengths: genuine "people like me" granularity at the individual level, scale and freshness on the China-to-US corridor, coverage of the downstream (salaries, visas) rather than just admission, and a self-sustaining contribution loop. Gaps: datapoints are isolated events never linked into per-person paths, so trajectories must be mentally stitched; heavy corridor and self-selection bias makes any computed rate uninterpretable; no map, no aggregate destination statistics; and the paywall meters exactly the browse-many-cases behavior comparison requires. It proves demand for cohort-conditioned evidence while storing it as unlinked, gated anecdotes.

## Comparison

|---|---|---|---|

| THE WUR | Scopus + reputation survey (33%) + self-reported | Institution | No |
| ARWU | Prizes + Web of Science bibliometrics | Institution | No |
| CSRankings | DBLP papers × curated CS faculty | Department | No |

| OSR | ORCID career trajectories | Country / city / institution, per origin nationality | Yes for researchers; partial otherwise — Arrival, Stay, No-return, Tier; population is ORCID-registered researchers |

## Design space

The six rankings and indices all measure institutions — their prestige, publications, prizes, or patents — and present one identical ordering to every visitor. None contains a single observed human trajectory, so the questions that dominate a relocation decision (where do people from my country go, do they stay, do they return) are structurally out of scope, not merely unimplemented. The two sources that do carry people-level evidence invert the weakness: LinkedIn holds a billion snapshots but no trajectories, no origin dimension, and no usable data on the mainland-Chinese cohort; 1point3acres holds cohort-matched trajectory fragments but as unlinked, corridor-biased, pay-metered anecdotes with no aggregate view.

The unoccupied cell is therefore: destinations ranked by observed migration and retention of scholars, conditioned on origin nationality, at country, city, and institution level. OSR fills it with linked ORCID career paths aggregated into three explainable metrics (Arrival, Stay, No-return) and a recursive Tier, browsable for free through a map, boards, and trajectory flows. What the benchmarks contribute is interaction discipline rather than data: QS/THE-grade low-friction entry (one board, one filter, instant drill-down), CSRankings/ARWU-grade methodology transparency, Nature Index's one-line-metric-plus-caveats packaging, and 1p3a's lesson that users want the evidence conditioned on a cohort resembling themselves. The known trade-off is LinkedIn's terminal affordance — anonymized aggregates can never end at a contactable person — which OSR must offset with maximal drill-down transparency into methodology and per-destination evidence.
