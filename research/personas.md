# Personas

Three personas grounded in the coded launch feedback (431 comments, `research/comments/themes.md`) and the author's own posts. P1 is primary and explicitly autobiographical: OSR began as the author's own high-school wish ("I wanted to click around with a mouse and instantly see the odds of settling down somewhere, or how to pick between two offers"), so the primary persona mirrors the author's origin story. This is autobiographical design in Neustaedter & Sengers' sense — the designer is a genuine user of the system — and it is acknowledged rather than hidden: it explains both the product's strengths (metric depth) and its blind spots (desktop-only, jargon-heavy), which the other two personas expose. P2 and P3 are the two strongest non-author user types in the data: the master's-level offer-holder (the most-repeated expansion request plus the single most-liked methodology comment) and the working professional planning an exit (the largest use-case theme, 23 comments, plus the socioeconomic feature-request cluster).

## P1 (primary) — Chen Rui, 20, CS undergraduate planning a direct PhD abroad

| | |
|---|---|
| Situation | Second-year CS student at a mid-tier Chinese university. Decent grades, one workshop paper in progress. Plans to skip the master's and apply to direct-PhD programs abroad; currently deciding *which countries* to shortlist before picking labs. |
| Goals | Compare countries on long-run settlement odds, not admission odds: if the PhD goes fine, where does it actually lead? Rank candidate institutions inside a country by whether alumni stay. Justify the shortlist to his parents with numbers instead of forum anecdotes. |
| Frustrations | Existing evidence is anecdotal and scattered ("Where is the data from? It feels like very few people compile statistics on this" — Data provenance). Aggregate rankings feel like repackaged prestige lists ("Isn't this clickbait? This is obviously just a university ranking" — Metric comprehension). Definitions matter to him and are under-explained: does a short trip home break No return? ("Short trips home to visit family shouldn't count?" — Metric comprehension). |
| Tech context | Desktop-first: laptop, external monitor, comfortable with dashboards and reading a methodology page. Found OSR via the Reddit write-up; uses a VPN routinely. Will read /info end to end if it exists. |

Grounding: author's motivation post (verbatim origin story); Data provenance (5); Metric comprehension (14); the author's own reply history answering exactly this persona's questions in-thread.

**Scenario.** Chen Rui has offers-in-spirit from labs in Germany, Australia, and Japan, and one evening opens OSR on his laptop to decide which two to pursue seriously. On /mobility he sets nationality to China and reads the country board: Australia shows the highest Stay, which surprises him because forum threads say the window closed after 2024. Before trusting it he opens /info and reads the methods notes on how Stay is computed, including the right-censoring rule — an arrival at a person's last observed stop is excluded from Stay and its denominator — which explains why the figures aren't inflated by recent arrivals. He drills into Germany, expecting strong numbers, and finds them lower than Australia's; he flags this as something to cross-check rather than accept, since /info documents the rules but publishes no per-destination sample sizes (the board's Arrival figure is a confidence-weighted mass, not a raw n). Switching to the city level, he compares Munich against Melbourne and Tokyo, then opens the trajectory flows to see where Chinese arrivals in each city move next. At the institution level he looks up his three candidate labs' universities and reads their R-tiers, treating R1 as "people who arrive here tend to stay and not return". One university he expected is missing; after a minute he notices the flow-threshold filter is on by default, toggles it off, and the university appears on page two. He screenshots the two boards, pastes them into his application-planning document, and drops Japan from the shortlist because its No return figure is far below the other two. Finally he bookmarks /ranking to revisit once the promised per-field breakdown ships, since a CS-specific tier would carry more weight with his advisor. The whole session takes forty minutes and replaces a week of forum reading.

## P2 — Lin Xiaoyu, 24, master's applicant comparing two offers

| | |
|---|---|
| Situation | Final-year undergraduate in finance at a Chinese university, holding two taught-master's offers: one in Australia, one in Germany. Chooses within six weeks. Not on a research track; the master's is a migration vehicle. |
| Goals | Pick the offer with the better realistic path to staying: post-study work rights, employer demand, long-run settlement. Wants OSR's numbers to apply to *her* — a master's student — not just PhD researchers. |
| Frustrations | Scope mismatch: "What the OP measures is the stay rate of PhD-track people, not bachelor's or master's students, right?" (126 likes, Metric comprehension) — she can't tell how much of OSR transfers to her case, and the repeated "waiting for the master's-level version" requests (Coverage requests) are hers too. Numbers clash with lived reports: "Australia is actually first? That data was fairly accurate before 2024, but now it seems really hard" (Plausibility doubts) and the UK cautionary tale — a friend "stayed on a PSW visa, mass-applied to eight hundred places, and still couldn't find a job meeting work-visa requirements" (Personal migration planning). And the site barely works where she lives: "Opening it on my phone, it seems the map can't be zoomed?" (Mobile experience). |
| Tech context | Phone-first: found OSR inside a rednote post, browses in the in-app webview, no VPN on mobile. Will not install anything or read long English methodology text; decides from boards and screenshots shared in group chats. |

Grounding: Coverage and feature expansion requests (11 — master's version asked repeatedly); Metric comprehension (14 — top-liked scope question, 126 likes); Plausibility and credibility doubts (14 — Australia/Germany quotes); Mobile experience and access problems (7); Personal migration planning and strategies (23 — UK PSW account).

**Scenario.** Lin Xiaoyu sees OSR in a rednote post a classmate tagged her in and opens the link on her phone during a commute. The map loads but won't pinch-zoom, half the board is cut off, and after two minutes she gives up and stars the post instead. That weekend she borrows her roommate's laptop and tries again on a full screen. She sets nationality to China and compares Australia and Germany side by side: Australia's Stay is clearly higher, which contradicts everything her group chats say about the post-2024 job market there. Unsure whether to trust it, she scrolls the comment section of the original post and finds the author's reply explaining that the data is ORCID researchers — mostly PhD-track people — and her heart sinks a little, because she is exactly the case the data doesn't cover. Still, she reasons the *relative* comparison may hold: if even researchers struggle to stay in Germany, a taught-master's graduate will struggle more. She drills into city level, compares Sydney against Munich, and checks No return to gauge how final the move tends to be. The Germany numbers look low in a way she can't explain, which matches a doubt she saw upvoted in the comments, so she treats OSR as one input among three rather than a verdict. She screenshots the country board, sends it to her offer-decision group chat with "not master's data, but directionally useful", and asks the group to sanity-check it against people they actually know. When someone in the chat asks where the numbers come from, she pastes the author's "ORCID Public Data Files 2025" reply. She sets a reminder to check back for the promised small-screen version and the master's-level breakdown before her acceptance deadline.

## P3 — Zhao Kai, 28, working professional planning an exit route

| | |
|---|---|
| Situation | Backend engineer at a Shenzhen tech firm, five years in, gay and not out at work. No research background. Treats a funded PhD or a master's-then-work-visa chain as candidate exit routes, but the decision variables are livability, legal protections, wages, and immigration difficulty — not academic prestige. |
| Goals | A first-pass shortlist of countries that are simultaneously realistic to enter and good to live in long-term: "I really want to know which places are good for sexual minorities and also easy to emigrate to" (76 likes). Strategy is sequenced, not optimized: "Step one: get out first, no matter where" (706 likes) — he needs OSR to tell him which first steps don't dead-end. |
| Frustrations | OSR's population isn't him: it covers researchers, and he wants "social security and welfare, real wages and consumption by industry" (27 likes, Coverage requests) and "a livability-destination ranking" (11 likes) layered on top; one commenter in his position sketched an entire counterpart tool for working professionals (Personal migration planning). He also distrusts what high Stay means: "people who overstay illegally can't go back, while those who get legal status will definitely visit home" (9 likes, ORCID-proxy validity). And he worries about the tool's survival: he watched the post get taken down six times and wants it "on X or GitHub, just don't promote it here" (Censorship anxiety, 20 likes) — a dead link is a dead plan. |
| Tech context | Desktop at home with a stable VPN; browses rednote on phone but does real research on PC. Comfortable with GitHub, keeps local copies of things that might disappear, asked whether the project is open source "so I don't lose track of it". |

Grounding: composite persona — the quotes are drawn from different commenters across the themes below (no single commenter said all of this), and the demographics (age 28, backend engineer, Shenzhen, five years' tenure) are assumed, not stated by any commenter. Personal migration planning and strategies (23 — the largest use-case theme; "get out first" 706 likes, LGBTQ quote 76 likes, professional-counterpart-tool comment); Coverage and feature expansion requests (11 — welfare/wages 27 likes, livability 11 likes); ORCID-as-migration-proxy validity (4); Censorship anxiety and self-protective framing (29); Open-source and offshore hosting requests (2).

**Scenario.** Zhao Kai finds OSR through the fourth re-upload of the rednote post, and the takedown count itself convinces him the content is worth saving, so his first act is bookmarking the .moe domain directly and checking whether there's a GitHub repo. That evening on his PC he opens /mobility, sets nationality to China, and reads the country board not as an academic ranking but as a revealed-preference signal: where do people who *can* choose end up staying? He filters his mental shortlist — Canada, Netherlands, Germany, Australia — and compares their Stay and No return figures, treating high No return as evidence that the move can be made permanent. Iraq's near-100% Stay stops him cold until he connects it to a comment he saw: small samples and trapped populations can masquerade as retention, so he decides to ignore any country with a thin flow. He opens the trajectory flows for the Netherlands to see whether arrivals later scatter onward or settle, since a country people transit through is not a country to bet on. What OSR cannot tell him — LGBTQ legal protections, tech salaries, cost of living — he pulls from other tabs, wishing the site layered even one socioeconomic signal onto the board. He drafts a two-path plan: a Dutch master's followed by the orientation-year visa, or applying to industry-adjacent PhD programs in Canada, and uses OSR's institution tiers only to break ties between otherwise similar schools. Before closing, he saves full-page screenshots of every board he used, because in his experience useful things on the Chinese internet disappear. He shares the X mirror, not the rednote post, with two close friends in the same situation. His verdict in the group chat: wrong population, right direction — the only tool that shows where leaving actually leads.
